SLIDE 1: Chapter 50 — Deadlock

LECTURE NARRATION
Deadlock is a state in which participants cannot progress because each waits for resources or actions that the others cannot provide without first progressing themselves. It differs from slow execution, starvation, and livelock. A system can be busy while making no useful progress, or idle while permanently blocked. Preventing deadlock requires a resource-acquisition policy and lifecycle design. Adding timeouts can reveal or limit a wait, but it does not automatically restore a consistent state or release every dependent resource.

At graduate level, do not treat this as a term to memorize. Identify the contract being described, the state or representation it constrains, and the assumptions that must remain true. SLIDE 4: 50.1 Deadlock conditions

LECTURE NARRATION
The classical resource model identifies four conditions associated with deadlock: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, and circular wait. Together they describe how a closed dependency cycle can become permanent under that model. The conditions are an analysis tool, not a claim that every concurrent failure is a lock cycle. Waiting for a notification that will never be sent, joining a thread while preventing its shutdown, or blocking a pipeline before starting its consumer can create related progress failures that deserve explicit dependency analysis.

SLIDE 6: 50.2 Mutual exclusion

LECTURE NARRATION
Mutual exclusion means some resource cannot be used by all participants simultaneously. A mutex is the obvious example, but a single-capacity communication slot or exclusive external transaction can also play a role. Removing unnecessary exclusivity can eliminate a dependency. Immutable shared data and independent per-thread state often need no exclusive lock. Where exclusivity is required for correctness, the design must address the other conditions instead of simply removing protection.

SLIDE 8: 50.3 Hold and wait

LECTURE NARRATION
Hold and wait occurs when a participant retains one resource while requesting another. A thread holding lock A while waiting for lock B creates an edge from its current ownership to its next dependency. Acquiring all required locks through a coordinated operation or releasing held resources before a retry can break this condition in suitable designs. Releasing and retrying requires care: state may change, so validation must be repeated and partial updates must not escape.

SLIDE 10: 50.4 No preemption

LECTURE NARRATION
No preemption means the resource cannot simply be taken away from its owner safely. An ordinary mutex cannot be forcibly unlocked by an unrelated thread without violating its contract. The owner may be halfway through an invariant-changing operation. This is why “kill the stuck thread” is not a general recovery strategy. The thread may leave shared state inconsistent and locks permanently unavailable. Process isolation and transactional recovery can provide stronger failure boundaries when forced termination is a requirement.

SLIDE 12: 50.5 Circular wait

LECTURE NARRATION
Circular wait exists when participants form a dependency cycle. Thread A holds lock X and waits for Y, while thread B holds Y and waits for X. Neither can reach the unlock that the other requires. A global lock order prevents this pattern when every participant follows it. The order must cover all relevant locks, including library callbacks and less obvious resources, or the apparent proof can omit an edge.

SLIDE 14: 50.6 Wait-for graphs

LECTURE NARRATION
A wait-for graph draws an edge from a waiting participant to the participant whose action or resource release it needs. A cycle indicates a potential or actual deadlock under the graph’s resource assumptions. For locks with one owner, the graph is straightforward. Multiple-instance resources, timeouts, and alternative progress paths require more careful interpretation. Record what each edge means and whether an external event can break it before declaring a permanent deadlock.

SLIDE 16: 50.7 Lock ordering

LECTURE NARRATION
Lock ordering assigns a consistent order and requires acquisition in that order. Two-account transfers can order accounts by a stable application identifier, or use a facility such as std::scoped_lock that applies a deadlock-avoiding multi-lock algorithm. Do not use built-in relational comparison of unrelated object pointers as a portable general ordering rule. Use stable identifiers or the appropriate library total-order facility when pointer ordering is genuinely needed. Also handle the same-object case before attempting to lock one nonrecursive mutex twice.

SLIDE 20: 50.9 Deadlock avoidance

LECTURE NARRATION
Avoidance examines current allocations and future needs to reject requests that could enter an unsafe state. Classical banker’s-algorithm reasoning requires known resource limits and maximum claims, which many general software workloads do not provide accurately. An unsafe state is not necessarily already deadlocked; it lacks a guaranteed completion sequence under the stated maximum-need assumptions. Distinguish admission control based on known budgets from prevention through a fixed lock order.

SLIDE 24: Worked example — Detecting a circular wait

LECTURE NARRATION
Thread A holds lock X and waits for Y, while thread B holds Y and waits for X. The wait-for graph contains A -> B and B -> A, a cycle. If neither lock can be preempted, the two threads cannot progress. Enforcing a global order such as always acquire X before Y removes this two-lock cycle.
