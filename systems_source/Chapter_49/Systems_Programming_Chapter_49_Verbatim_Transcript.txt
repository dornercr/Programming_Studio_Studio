SLIDE 1: Chapter 49 — Producer–Consumer Systems

LECTURE NARRATION
A producer–consumer system separates creation of work from processing of work. A bounded queue connects the two rates and applies backpressure when producers get ahead. Its correctness requires more than a mutex around a container: it needs predicates, closure semantics, ownership rules, and a way for every waiter to terminate. The queue in this chapter stores integers in preallocated slots. That keeps allocation and throwing element moves out of push and pop , making the synchronization argument visible. A generic production queue must additionally define exception guarantees for its element type.

SLIDE 4: 49.1 Producers

LECTURE NARRATION
Producers create items and attempt to transfer them into the queue. A successful push transfers responsibility to the queue. A rejected push after closure leaves the caller responsible for whatever resource the item represents. Production rate should not be assumed lower than consumption rate. Bursts, slow downstream services, and failures can make the queue fill. Bounded capacity converts that condition into an explicit wait or rejection instead of unbounded memory growth.

SLIDE 6: 49.2 Consumers

LECTURE NARRATION
Consumers wait for an available item, remove it under the queue’s protection, and process it outside the queue lock. Processing outside the lock lets other participants enqueue and dequeue while the current item is being handled. A consumer must distinguish temporarily empty from permanently closed-and-drained. Returning an empty result merely because no item is available at one instant would incorrectly terminate a worker while producers are still active.

SLIDE 8: 49.3 Shared buffers

LECTURE NARRATION
The shared buffer stores items not yet transferred to consumers. A ring buffer uses a fixed array with head and tail indexes. Count distinguishes empty from full when indexes alone are ambiguous. The invariant includes 0 <= count <= capacity , valid head and tail indexes, and a relationship between occupied slots and logical order. All fields that participate in that invariant use the same mutex in this implementation.

SLIDE 10: 49.4 Bounded buffers

LECTURE NARRATION
A bounded buffer has a fixed maximum number of queued items. It limits queue storage and provides a natural point for backpressure. It does not by itself limit all system resources: producers may hold items before pushing, and consumers may hold items while processing. Budget the complete pipeline. If each of 100 workers can retain a large item, a small queue does not imply a small total memory footprint. Capacity controls one ownership stage, not every live object in the process.

SLIDE 12: 49.5 Buffer-full condition

LECTURE NARRATION
A producer waits while the queue is full and still open. Its predicate is closed || count < capacity . Including closure ensures that shutdown can release producers that would otherwise wait forever for capacity. After waking, the producer checks closure before inserting. A notification does not reserve a slot for a particular producer; another producer may acquire the mutex first. The predicate loop handles that competition.

SLIDE 14: 49.6 Buffer-empty condition

LECTURE NARRATION
A consumer waits while the queue is empty and open. Its predicate is closed || count > 0 . If the queue is closed but still contains items, the consumer drains them under the policy used here. When closed and empty, pop returns no item, signaling normal completion. This is drain-on-close , not immediate cancellation. An aborting queue would need a different contract for queued and in-flight items.

SLIDE 16: 49.7 Mutex synchronization

LECTURE NARRATION
One mutex protects slots, indexes, count, and closure. The queue does not expose references to occupied slots after unlocking. Instead it copies an integer out while protected, then returns ownership of that value. The mutex also supplies visibility between the producer’s slot write and the consumer’s slot read. No extra atomic count is needed in this locked implementation. Adding atomics to already-protected fields can complicate the design without improving correctness.

SLIDE 18: 49.8 Condition-variable synchronization

LECTURE NARRATION
One condition variable represents the possibility of items becoming available; another represents capacity becoming available. Push notifies a consumer after insertion, and pop notifies a producer after removal. Closure notifies both groups. The predicates remain authoritative. Notifications happen after unlocking in this implementation, but state changes occur under the mutex. Queue destruction is permitted only after all producers and consumers have stopped using it; close is not an automatic join operation.

SLIDE 24: Worked example — Why two predicates matter

LECTURE NARRATION
A bounded queue has at least two logically distinct conditions: not-empty for consumers and not-full for producers. Using the same mutex is fine, but each side waits for the predicate that enables its operation. Notifications should be issued after state changes that can make the opposite predicate true.
