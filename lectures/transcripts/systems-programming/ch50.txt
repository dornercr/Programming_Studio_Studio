1. No progress has more than one cause
Problem and first experiment

SAY
Two threads can both be idle forever because each holds a lock the other needs. That is a deadlock under the ordinary lock model. A different thread may starve while others keep acquiring a resource, or participants may repeatedly retry without completing useful work, called livelock. Distinguish these cases before adding timeouts. A timeout can expose a long wait but does not restore a half-updated invariant or safely remove a lock from its owner. The first debugging question is who is waiting for which action and whether that action can occur. Draw those dependencies, including joins, pipe capacity, and callbacks as well as mutexes.

2. Describe the assumptions behind a wait-for cycle
Problem and first experiment

SAY
A wait-for graph connects a blocked participant to whoever must act before it can continue. For ordinary locks with one owner, A waiting for B's lock creates a clear edge from A to B. A closed cycle with no release path can show deadlock. But a graph for multiple-instance resources or timed alternative operations needs more careful interpretation. An edge is not enough unless we know what can remove it. State whether resources can be preempted, whether a participant can abandon its request, and whether an external event can let it finish. Those assumptions turn an attractive diagram into an actual progress argument.

SAY

4. Acquire the whole distinct lock set together
Problem and first experiment

SAY
Two workers rotate the same three values in opposite directions one hundred times each. Every rotation acquires all three distinct mutexes through scoped_lock before changing the array. Left and right rotations are inverse operations, so after both joined workers finish, the output is values=7,8,9 total=24 regardless of their interleaving. No intermediate rotation is visible to the other worker while the locks are held. This demonstrates coordinated multi-lock acquisition, not a reason to use three locks for every array. Since all shown operations need the whole state, one mutex would be simpler here. Multiple locks become relevant when other legitimate operations use independent regions.

8. Chapter 50 — Deadlock
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
Deadlock is a state in which participants cannot progress because each waits for resources or actions that the others cannot provide without first progressing themselves. It differs from slow execution, starvation, and livelock. A system can be busy while making no useful progress, or idle while permanently blocked. Preventing deadlock requires a resource-acquisition policy and lifecycle design. Adding timeouts can reveal or limit a wait, but it does not automatically restore a consistent state or release every dependent resource.

10. Deadlock conditions
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
The classical resource model identifies four conditions associated with deadlock: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, and circular wait. Together they describe how a closed dependency cycle can become permanent under that model. The conditions are an analysis tool, not a claim that every concurrent failure is a lock cycle. Waiting for a notification that will never be sent, joining a thread while preventing its shutdown, or blocking a pipeline before starting its consumer can create related progress failures that deserve explicit dependency analysis.

In plain English: This is a coordination rule that helps multiple workers share information without stepping on each other's work.

11. Mutual exclusion
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
Mutual exclusion means some resource cannot be used by all participants simultaneously. A mutex is the obvious example, but a single-capacity communication slot or exclusive external transaction can also play a role. Removing unnecessary exclusivity can eliminate a dependency. Immutable shared data and independent per-thread state often need no exclusive lock. Where exclusivity is required for correctness, the design must address the other conditions instead of simply removing protection.

12. Hold and wait
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
Hold and wait occurs when a participant retains one resource while requesting another. A thread holding lock A while waiting for lock B creates an edge from its current ownership to its next dependency. Acquiring all required locks through a coordinated operation or releasing held resources before a retry can break this condition in suitable designs. Releasing and retrying requires care: state may change, so validation must be repeated and partial updates must not escape.

13. No preemption
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
No preemption means the resource cannot simply be taken away from its owner safely. An ordinary mutex cannot be forcibly unlocked by an unrelated thread without violating its contract. The owner may be halfway through an invariant-changing operation. This is why “kill the stuck thread” is not a general recovery strategy. The thread may leave shared state inconsistent and locks permanently unavailable. Process isolation and transactional recovery can provide stronger failure boundaries when forced termination is a requirement.

14. Circular wait
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
Circular wait exists when participants form a dependency cycle. Thread A holds lock X and waits for Y, while thread B holds Y and waits for X. Neither can reach the unlock that the other requires. A global lock order prevents this pattern when every participant follows it. The order must cover all relevant locks, including library callbacks and less obvious resources, or the apparent proof can omit an edge.

15. Wait-for graphs
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
A wait-for graph draws an edge from a waiting participant to the participant whose action or resource release it needs. A cycle indicates a potential or actual deadlock under the graph’s resource assumptions. For locks with one owner, the graph is straightforward. Multiple-instance resources, timeouts, and alternative progress paths require more careful interpretation. Record what each edge means and whether an external event can break it before declaring a permanent deadlock.

16. Lock ordering
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
Lock ordering assigns a consistent order and requires acquisition in that order. Two-account transfers can order accounts by a stable application identifier, or use a facility such as std::scoped_lock that applies a deadlock-avoiding multi-lock algorithm. Do not use built-in relational comparison of unrelated object pointers as a portable general ordering rule. Use stable identifiers or the appropriate library total-order facility when pointer ordering is genuinely needed. Also handle the same-object case before attempting to lock one nonrecursive mutex twice.

17. Deadlock prevention
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
Prevention structurally breaks at least one condition. Examples include avoiding nested locks, using a global order, making state immutable, or acquiring a required set with a coordinated locking algorithm. Prevention can reduce concurrency or require redesign, but it gives a strong argument before deployment. A simple lock hierarchy documented and enforced in code is often more reliable than a collection of ad hoc timeouts added after hangs appear.

18. Deadlock avoidance
Systems mechanisms and source lecture

SAY
Avoidance examines current allocations and future needs to reject requests that could enter an unsafe state. Classical banker’s-algorithm reasoning requires known resource limits and maximum claims, which many general software workloads do not provide accurately. An unsafe state is not necessarily already deadlocked; it lacks a guaranteed completion sequence under the stated maximum-need assumptions. Distinguish admission control based on known budgets from prevention through a fixed lock order.

25. Break a condition without breaking the invariant
Reason about the implementation

SAY
The classical lock-resource analysis combines mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no safe preemption, and circular wait. Preventing deadlock means structurally breaking a necessary condition in the relevant model. Removing a mutex from shared mutable data might eliminate a wait while introducing a data race, so that is not a valid repair. A better option may use immutable data, avoid nested acquisition, impose one global order, or acquire the required set through a coordinated operation. Choose a change that preserves the protected invariant as well as progress. The list of conditions is useful because it points to design choices, not because memorizing four phrases diagnoses every hang automatically.

Reason about the implementation

28. Joining while locked creates a different cycle
Reason about the implementation

SAY
A deadlock can involve a join even if no thread tries to take two mutexes. Suppose the parent owns the queue mutex and joins a worker. The worker needs that mutex to observe closure and return. The parent waits for the worker, and the worker waits for the parent to unlock. Draw both kinds of edge instead of searching only for opposite lock order. The repair is to establish closure while protected, release the lock, notify as required, and then join. A similar dependency appears when a shell waits for a pipe producer before starting its consumer. Progress analysis must include every blocking operation the participants rely on.

44. Unsafe allocation is not necessarily current deadlock
Recall and transfer

SAY
Imagine three identical resources. Two participants each hold one and each may still need two more. Only one resource remains free, so neither maximum claim can be fully satisfied first. Under that maximum-need model, the state is unsafe: there is no guaranteed completion sequence. It is not necessarily already deadlocked, because neither participant may have issued a blocking request yet, or actual needs may be smaller than the maximum. Avoidance policies reject such risky admissions when their guarantees require a safe sequence. That differs from prevention through a fixed lock order. The analysis is useful only when capacities and maximum claims are meaningful and known.

45. Make a progress argument part of code review
Recall and transfer

SAY
Before approving a blocking operation, list what its caller still owns and who can satisfy the wait. If the required participant needs one of those retained resources, inspect the dependency cycle. Include locks, queue slots, join ownership, terminal relationships, and external callbacks. Timeouts may bound waiting, but recovery must still release resources safely and preserve or explicitly abandon partial work. Killing a thread that owns a mutex is not a general recovery plan. Prefer a clear hierarchy or simpler ownership boundary when possible. Tests should verify completion under demanding schedules, while the documented acquisition and shutdown policy explains why the design has a path to progress.
