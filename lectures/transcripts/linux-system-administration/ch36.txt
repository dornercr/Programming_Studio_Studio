4. Change plan

Requirement. What observable outcome is needed, and who approves it?

Scope. Which hosts, services, files, identities, and network paths can change? Which must remain untouched?

Preconditions. What access, backups, capacity, dependencies, and maintenance approvals are required? How will you recover when the ordinary management path fails?

Before-state evidence. Record the current revision, configuration, affected service state, and client-visible behavior. Preserve the exact files needed for a rollback rather than reconstructing them from memory.

Candidate validation. Record syntax checks, fixture tests, peer review, and known limitations. An unchecked condition remains unchecked; a test passing on another version is not identical evidence.

Application. List the minimum ordered changes. Establish explicit stop conditions. Keep secrets out of command history and change logs.

Verification. Test the declared outcome from the intended client path and inspect the relevant logs and metrics. Include a negative test when rejection is part of the requirement.

Rollback and closeout. State what triggers rollback, who authorizes it, and how configuration and data compatibility will be restored. Record the observed final state, any exceptions, and the next owner.

5. Incident timeline

Use one row per observation or action, with a consistent time basis. Useful fields are time, source, observed fact, hypothesis, test, result, action owner, and approval. An interpretation belongs in the hypothesis field until evidence supports it.

Preserve the first failure evidence when feasible. Repeated retries, reboots, and overwritten configuration can destroy the distinction between the initial fault and changes made during response. Record those later changes as part of the timeline rather than pretending the environment remained constant.

SOURCE: EPUB/text/ch038.xhtml — Incident timeline

6. Root-cause analysis

Begin with the user-visible impact and the verified sequence of events. Identify the triggering change, failed mechanism, contributing conditions, detection gap, and recovery constraints. Distinguish a proximal cause—such as a failed reload—from the process conditions that allowed it to escape detection.

A useful analysis says what is still unknown and what evidence would resolve it. Avoid blaming a person’s identity instead of explaining the system and decision context. Assign corrective actions to owners with due dates and an observable completion criterion. “Be more careful” is not a testable corrective action.

SOURCE: EPUB/text/ch038.xhtml — Root-cause analysis

7. Restore record

Record the selected backup, its data timestamp, integrity evidence, recovery environment, identity and key dependencies, restore start, service acceptance time, and tested data state. Explain whether the original service was isolated during the verification.

Compare the actual results with the declared recovery-point and recovery-time objectives. Record steps that required undocumented knowledge. Revise the recovery procedure and repeat the affected part of the exercise instead of accepting a one-time lucky recovery.

SOURCE: EPUB/text/ch038.xhtml — Restore record

8. Production handover

The receiving operator should be able to answer: what does the service promise, which dependencies can break it, where is its effective configuration, what is allowed to write its data, who owns its certificate, how does its alert reach a person, and how is it restored without its original host?

If a question has no answer, name the open requirement and owner. The handover should transfer operational understanding, not merely a folder of command transcripts.
